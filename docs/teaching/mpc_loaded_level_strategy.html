MPC Loaded Level Strategy

A beginner's guide · no jargon assumed · read the pages in order

There is one idea underneath this whole method, and everything else is machinery built on top of it.

You are trading other traders' stop losses.

Every trader who buys puts a stop loss somewhere below where they bought. Every trader who sells puts one above. Those stops are real orders sitting in the market, waiting. When enough of them pile up in one place, the market has a reason to go there and collect them — and once it has, it very often turns around and goes the other way.

So the whole skill is answering one question: where are the stops?

The mistake almost everyone makes

Most people are taught that there is money resting above every high and below every low. If that were true your chart would have a hundred targets on it and you would have no idea which to trade. The model refuses that assumption outright:

If liquidity sat above every high and below every low, every level would be a target and none of them would be. That is where most people get stuck: a chart covered in highs and lows, and no way to choose between them.

Most highs and lows have nothing at them. The market has to show you which ones are worth anything. That test is the next page, and it is the single most important thing in this document.

A word you will see everywhere here. When we say a level is LOADED, we mean traders have committed money there and their stops are sitting behind it. When we say a level is EMPTY, we mean the market has already been there, taken those stops, and left. Loaded and empty are the only two words you need.

What you will be able to do by the end

  1. Look at a bare chart and mark the two or three levels that actually matter.
  2. Recognise a complete setup as it builds, step by step.
  3. Know exactly where your entry, your stop and your target go — and why each is there.
  4. Know the six situations where the correct action is to do nothing.

The MPC Loaded Level strategy. The rules below are written to be followed exactly as stated; the measurements on page 7 are our own and are early-stage.

Loaded, or empty?

This is the test. Everything else follows from it.

A level is LOADED when price came back to it, held it, and walked away.

That is the whole test. Price has to return. If price made a high once and never went back near it, nothing happened there — nobody committed money, so there are no stops. It is just a high.

Left: price returned and held — the level is loaded. Right: price went through and kept going — the level is now empty.

Why you care about each one

A LOADED level

Has money behind it. You use it two ways:

Smash it → entry. When price finally breaks a loaded level, all those traders get stopped out at once. That is the fuel for the move. You join in the moment it happens.

Aim at it → target. The stops sitting there are what pulls price toward it. That is why your target is a level and not a round number.

An EMPTY level

Has nothing behind it. You use it one way, and it is the most important job in the model:

Hide your stop behind it. The market has already collected what was there. It has no errand back down. So your stop, tucked just past it, is in the one place on the chart the market is least interested in.

Trust the highs and lows the market has already defended. Those are the only ones worth marking.

The rule that keeps your chart clean. If price never came back and held it, do not draw a line on it. This one rule is the difference between a chart with three meaningful levels on it and a chart with forty meaningless ones.

The setup, step by step

Six steps. Shown as a buy. A sell is the exact mirror — turn the page upside down and every word still applies.

  • Price sweeps an old low and walks away. That low is now empty. Two things just happened: you only look for buys from here on, and you already know where your stop will live.
  • A level loads. Price bounces, then pulls back and makes a low that holds above an earlier one. Buyers are now in, with stops underneath. Mark this level. This is where you will buy. Then leave it alone.
  • Inducement. Price pushes up and takes out a small high. More buyers pile in, late. They are fuel for what comes next.
  • The target loads. Price makes a high that holds against earlier highs, then turns away. Sellers are now committed up there with stops above them. This step is the gate — see the box below.
  • Entry. Price sells back down and stabs through your level from step 2. Buy the moment it goes. Do not wait for the candle to close. Do not hunt a better price inside it.
  • Stop and target. Stop just below the empty level from step 1. Target the loaded high from step 4.
Step 4 is the gate, and it is the step beginners skip. If there is no loaded level above to aim at, there is no trade — no matter how good steps 1 to 3 looked. Say it as a rule: no engineered liquidity, no setup. Without step 4 you are just buying a dip and hoping.

The setup, step by step — SHORT

The same drawing turned upside down. Every step is mirrored; nothing else changes.

Drawn, not replayed — a teaching illustration. This is the long diagram on the previous page, flipped, so the two are provably the same sequence.

  1. Price sweeps an old high and walks away. That spike is now empty. There is nothing left above it to pull price back — which is why it is safe to hide a stop there. From here you are looking for sells only.
  2. A level loads. Price pulls back and prints a high that comes up to an earlier high, holds without breaking it, then moves away. Sellers are now positioned below it with their stops above. Mark it and leave it alone.
  3. Inducement. Price drops and takes out a minor low. More sellers pile in. They are the fuel.
  4. The target is built. Price prints a low that holds against earlier lows and turns away. That low is now loaded. No loaded level below, no trade — however good everything else looks.
  5. Entry. Price rallies back and stabs the loaded high from step 2. Sell the moment it is taken. Stop just above the empty level from step 1; target the loaded low from step 4.
The mistake to watch for. Selling below a loaded high because the chart already looks weak. This model sells above highs and buys below lows. If you are in before the level is taken, you are the liquidity.

Where the big reward-to-risk actually comes from. Not from picking better targets — from finding the same setup on a smaller timeframe, where the empty level is closer and the stop is a fraction of the size. The target does not move. The stop shrinks. Everything else is timing.

How to practise it

  1. Mark loaded and empty on a blank chart. No entries at all. Say out loud why each mark is what it is.
  2. Find the empty level first, every time. If you cannot say where your stop goes, you have not found the setup yet.
  3. Then find the target. No loaded level on the far side means close the chart. That is the step that turns a pattern into a trade.
  4. The entry is last. It is the easiest part and the one everybody starts with.

Reading strength

All loaded levels are worth marking. Some are worth much more than others.

Once you can spot a loaded level, the next skill is judging how much is sitting behind it. Three things tell you, and all three are just careful looking — no indicator required.

1 · How long price spent on one side of it

The longer price hovers above a low without breaking it, the more people have bought there and the more stops have stacked underneath. A level that price grinds above for five or six hours has a far deeper pool beneath it than one that held for twenty minutes.

Time spent above a level is the measure of what is stacked beneath it. A low that price grinds above for five or six hours has been bought into over and over, and every one of those buyers left a stop underneath.

Judge it in candles, not in clock time. Twenty candles of grinding is the same signal on a 1-minute chart as on an hourly one. That is what makes this work on any timeframe.

2 · How many times it held

Once is a level. Twice is a better level. Three or four times and a lot of people are watching it. He counts out loud on the chart — "once, twice, three times" — because each touch adds another layer of orders.

3 · How violently it was taken

When the sweep finally comes, look at how fast it happened. Two big candles erasing thirteen hours of patient grinding is a far stronger signal than a slow drift through.

The collection is fast and the build is slow. Half a day of grinding gets swept by one or two candles — which is why you must already be positioned when it happens, not deciding.

Where these levels usually are

You do not have to hunt randomly. Loaded levels cluster in predictable places:

Level Why traders are there
Yesterday's high and low Watched by almost everybody. Stops stack just past them.
The previous session's high and low Asia's range, then London's — each one becomes the next session's target.
Equal highs / equal lows Two or more touches at the same price. Obvious to everyone, which is the point.
The previous candle's high or low On any timeframe. When a candle closes leaving the one before it un-swept, the next candle often goes and takes it.
Round numbers People place orders at tidy prices.
Sweeps like to happen at the start of something. A new hourly candle, a session open, the minutes after a news release. If your level gets taken right as London opens, that is not a coincidence — it is the most common time for it.

When to do nothing

This deserves more of your attention than entries do.

Most of the time, the right answer is that there is no trade. That is not you failing to find one — it is the model working. Here are the six situations where you stand down.

The situation Why you stay out
1 Nothing to aim at No loaded level on the far side. You have no target, so you have no trade, however good the entry looks. "When you don't see liquidity, we wait."
2 Liquidity still sitting in the way There is an un-swept pool between you and your target — often off to the left of the screen. Price will go and take that first. Wait until it has.
3 Price is on the wrong side of the pool You want to buy below a low that has not been swept yet. Until price actually goes below it, every rally is a trap. Do nothing and stop caring about the wiggles.
4 The stop would be too wide If the empty level is far away, your stop is huge and the reward no longer justifies it. First try dropping one timeframe to find a closer one. If you still cannot, skip the trade.
5 A quiet inside day After a day that swept both yesterday's high and its low, the next day often just chops around inside that range. Poor conditions. Trade smaller or not at all.
6 A move that came from nothing If a rally started at no level you had marked, it is not a real move — it is bait. Do not chase it. If anything, it is a clue to trade the other way.

Two traps worth naming

A reaction at your level is not a signal

Price will often bounce off a level without the setup completing. That bounce is not the trade starting — it is more orders being built for later. Wait for the full sequence, not the first flicker.

Do not buy toward liquidity that is still above you

If there is an un-swept high overhead, price wants to go and take it. Buying into that is buying just before the market collects the stops of everyone who did the same. Let it get taken first, then look.

How often should this actually fire? The really good ones come a couple of times a week at best, sometimes once or twice a month. If you are finding several a day, you have almost certainly loosened the rules without noticing.

After you are in

The trade is expected to go against you. This page is why people lose on a setup that eventually worked.

Here is the sequence that plays out again and again after entry:

  1. You get a fast move in your favour. You are in profit quickly. It feels great.
  2. Price comes all the way back — often making a new extreme first. This is the market inducing one last batch of traders before it goes. It is normal. It is expected.
  3. Then the real move happens and runs to your target.
The single most expensive mistake in this model. At step 1, feeling clever, people move their stop up to lock in the profit. At step 2, that new stop gets hit. Then they watch step 3 happen without them.

Do not move your stop because the trade went your way. This is the single most common way a good setup turns into a scratch, and it costs you the winners specifically.

So when may you move it?

When something actually happens on the chart — not when a certain amount of profit appears. The trigger is a level being taken. Once price has run the next pool of liquidity on the way to your target, that part of the job is done and you may protect the trade.

Once the level you were aiming at has actually been taken, the reason you were in the trade is spent — manage the risk. Put the other way: the stop only moves once the trade has run a meaningful distance your way, never on a few points of drift.

Taking profit

Targets are levels, not numbers you invent. Take the first one at the nearest loaded level, and if you want to hold a piece for a bigger one, aim at the loaded level on the higher timeframe. Exactly how much you take at each is personal. The one firm rule: do not bother banking at less than about 3 times your risk.

If it stops you out, the idea may still be right. As long as your target is still sitting there un-swept, the direction has not been proven wrong — you were just early. Wait for a new level to load and take it again.

What we actually measured

Read this page before you risk money. It is the part no video gives you.

Across sixteen videos teaching this model there is no win rate, no number of trades, and no equity curve — not once. So we coded the sequence ourselves and ran it over eight years of gold data to see what is actually there.

XAUUSD, 15-minute bars, 186,759 bars, September 2018 to August 2026. Every setup is scored against random trades with the same direction, the same stop distance and the same target distance — because on a market that tripled in price, any buying rule looks clever unless you check it against luck.

Side Trades Won Random did Edge Verdict
Buys 49 34.7% 22.9% +11.8 pts Real, but a small sample
Sells 80 21.2% 20.9% +0.4 pts No better than random

What that means in plain English

  1. The buy side showed a genuine edge — it won noticeably more often than luck with the same stop and target. After a realistic spread it still made money.
  2. The sell side showed nothing at all. Identical detection, no edge. We do not yet know whether that is the model or our code.
  3. 49 trades in eight years is a very small sample. That is about six a year. A result this size is a hint, not a proof.
  4. The edge only appeared on the high-reward setups. When we allowed trades with a reward smaller than twice the risk, the advantage over random vanished completely.
Point 4 is the useful one, and it matches the rule as written. Wait for the higher-probability setups and refuse anything under roughly 3:1. Our numbers agree: the patience is not a preference, it appears to be where the whole edge lives.

Do not read this page as a green light

One instrument, one timeframe, one set of settings, a small number of trades, and a simplified way of deciding whether the stop or the target was hit first. This is early work. Nobody should be sizing up on this yet.

Things nobody has pinned down

Honest gaps, so you know what you are judging by eye rather than by rule: how many candles of building is "enough"; how close a return counts as "holding" the level; how long a level stays empty before it matters again; and what hours of the day to trade — the model skips setups outside a defined trading window, and that window has never been pinned down.

Your checklist

Print this page. Work down it in order, every single time.

Before you look for a trade

  • Start on the higher timeframe. If nothing is there, do not drop lower.
  • Mark only levels that price returned to and held.
  • Find the empty level — the one price smashed and left. That is where risk can sit.
  • Ask: is there a loaded level on the far side to aim at? If no, stop here.
  • Ask: is anything un-swept in the way? If yes, wait for it.

The setup

  • 1 · An old level was swept and price walked away.
  • 2 · A level has loaded. It is marked. I have not touched it since.
  • 3 · A minor high or low was taken — latecomers are in.
  • 4 · A level has loaded on the far side. This is my target.

The trade

  • Entry: the instant my level from step 2 is stabbed. No waiting, no better price.
  • Stop: just past the empty level. Written down before I click.
  • Target: the loaded level from step 4.
  • Reward is at least 3× my risk — or I do not take it.

Once I am in

  • I expect it to come back against me. That is normal.
  • I will not move my stop because I am in profit.
  • I will only move it once a level has actually been taken.
  • If I am stopped and the target is still un-swept, the idea stands. I wait for the next one.

Stop and walk away if…

  • There is nothing to aim at.
  • The stop would be too wide to justify.
  • It is a quiet inside day.
  • I am about to chase a move that started from nothing.

You are trading other traders' stop losses.
Smash a loaded level to get in. Hide behind an empty one. Aim at another loaded one.
